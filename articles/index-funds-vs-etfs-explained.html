The Mechanics

Index funds vs. ETFs: what's actually different structurally

Both can track the exact same index, hold the exact same underlying stocks, and charge a similar fee — yet people talk about them as if they're interchangeable. Structurally, they're not quite the same thing. Here's where the difference is cosmetic and where it can actually matter.

"Index fund" describes a strategy: hold a basket of securities that mirrors a market index, like a broad stock index, instead of trying to pick winners. That strategy can be delivered through two different legal wrappers — a traditional open-end mutual fund, or an exchange-traded fund (ETF). Most of what people call "index funds" in casual conversation are actually the mutual fund version; ETFs are a separate wrapper that can run the identical strategy. The strategy is often the same. The mechanics of buying, selling, and being taxed on it are not.

How they're priced and traded

Traditional index fundETF
When you can tradeOnce per day, after markets closeContinuously, during exchange hours
Price you getEnd-of-day net asset value (NAV) — set once, everyone gets the same price that dayWhatever the market price is at that moment, which trades very close to (but not always exactly) NAV
How you buy itDirectly through the fund company or a brokerage, often with a minimum investmentLike a stock, through any brokerage, no minimum beyond one share (or a fraction, where supported)

The traditional fund's single daily price is calculated after the market closes, based on the closing value of everything it holds. An ETF, by contrast, trades on an exchange all day, with a market price driven by ordinary buying and selling — for large, liquid ETFs tracking major indexes, that market price typically stays within a hair of the fund's actual underlying value, kept tight by professional traders who profit from correcting any gap. For small or thinly traded ETFs, that gap — a premium or discount to NAV — can be more noticeable.

The redemption mechanism, and why it affects taxes

This is the part that gets skipped most often. When investors sell out of a traditional mutual fund faster than others are buying in, the fund manager may need to sell some of the fund's underlying holdings to raise cash for those redemptions. If those holdings have gained value since purchase, selling them can trigger a capital gain — one that gets distributed across all remaining shareholders, whether or not they personally sold anything that year.

ETFs largely sidestep this through a different plumbing system: large institutional players called authorized participants create and redeem ETF shares by exchanging baskets of the underlying securities directly, "in kind," rather than the fund trading them for cash. In tax systems where an in-kind exchange like this isn't itself a taxable event (notably the U.S.), this tends to make ETFs distribute fewer unexpected capital gains than a comparable mutual fund holding the same securities. Whether — and how much — this matters depends entirely on your local tax rules and the account type you're using; inside a tax-advantaged retirement account, this difference is often irrelevant either way, since gains inside the account usually aren't taxed annually regardless of wrapper.

Where the difference is mostly cosmetic

For a long-term, buy-and-hold investor using a widely tracked index — a broad domestic stock index, for example — held inside a tax-advantaged account, the fund-versus-ETF choice often comes down to convenience rather than economics. Expense ratios for large, popular index funds and their ETF equivalents are frequently close to each other. If your platform makes one easier to set up as an automatic recurring purchase, or your retirement plan simply only offers the mutual fund version, that practical reality usually outweighs the structural difference.

Where it can actually matter

  • Taxable accounts, in a tax system where fund capital-gains distributions are taxed annually: the ETF's in-kind mechanism can meaningfully reduce unplanned tax bills over time.
  • Wanting exact dollar amounts: traditional funds let you invest a precise sum (say, exactly $500) at end-of-day NAV; ETFs require buying in shares, though fractional-share investing at many brokerages now closes this gap.
  • Wanting intraday control: only ETFs let you place a limit order at a specific price or trade mid-day; mutual funds only ever transact at that day's single closing NAV.
  • Very small or niche ETFs: low trading volume can mean wider bid-ask spreads, which function as an extra, easy-to-miss cost on each trade — worth checking before buying an obscure or newly launched ETF.
